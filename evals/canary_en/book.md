# Preface: The Staircase

When I was nine, my grandmother taught me to knit by making me unravel my first scarf three times. I resented it then. I now think it was the best lesson in learning I ever received. This book argues that the feeling of fast progress is a poor guide to actual learning, and that the "long way round" is often the shorter road.

The book has three parts. Part One (Chapters 1 and 2) shows that fluency and learning are different things. Part Two (Chapters 3 and 4) explains what makes difficulty useful and when it stops being useful. I should say at the start that I am a teacher, not a laboratory scientist, and I will say so again whenever it matters.

# Chapter 1: The Fluency Trap

## Feeling is not knowing

Most people judge how well they have learned something by how easy it feels. Rereading a chapter feels productive because the words look familiar. I call this the fluency trap: the ease of processing is mistaken for the strength of memory. A common view among study-skills books is that highlighting and rereading are the best ways to prepare for an exam. I will argue that this view is wrong, though I do not claim it is stupid; it is simply what feels right.

## A classroom comparison

In 2017 I ran a comparison in my own school with 212 students aged fifteen and sixteen. Half studied a passage by rereading it three times. The other half read it once and then spent the same time trying to write down everything they could remember. A week later the second group recalled 18 percent more of the passage. The students who had reread predicted, before the test, that they would do better. They were wrong.

I must be honest about the limits of this comparison. The students were not randomly assigned: I let each class choose its method, and the two classes had different teachers. A different design might shrink the gap or erase it.

# Chapter 2: Tests Are Not Only Tests

## Retrieval as a way of learning

Trying to recall something strengthens the memory more than reading it again does. This is sometimes called the testing effect, and it is the best-supported idea in this book; researchers have documented it in many settings, though I will not cite specific studies here, and the book does not rely on any single one.

## Why it works

My explanation, which is a hypothesis rather than a result, is that effortful retrieval forces the mind to rebuild the memory, and rebuilding leaves a stronger trace than recognising. I also suspect that retrieval shows learners what they do not yet know, which rereading hides.

## A caution about stakes

Some readers worry that constant testing makes students anxious. In my classes I used low-stakes quizzes that did not count toward grades, and anxiety did not seem to rise. That is an impression from one school, not a finding.

# Chapter 3: Desirable Difficulty

## The idea

Some difficulties help learning and some do not. I call a difficulty desirable when it makes the learner work at the material itself: spacing practice over days, mixing problem types, writing answers before seeing them. A difficulty is undesirable when it is only an obstacle, such as unclear instructions or a noisy room.

## Spacing

In my school we moved a unit of mathematics from five consecutive days to five sessions spread over three weeks. Test scores on the unit rose by an average of 7 points out of 100. Students complained that the spaced version felt harder and less organised, and about a third of them said they preferred the old schedule.

## The limit of the idea

Desirable difficulty has a ceiling. If a task is far beyond the learner's current knowledge, the struggle teaches little. I originally wrote in my notes that "the harder, the better", and that was a mistake. The useful zone is difficulty that the learner can overcome with effort and feedback.

# Chapter 4: Making the Long Way Practical

## Three habits

I recommend three habits: close the book and write what you remember before checking; space study sessions so that each topic returns after a few days; and mix problem types within a session rather than finishing one type at a time.

## When not to use them

These habits are poorly suited to cramming a fact you need for tomorrow and will never need again. They also assume the learner has some foundation; a complete beginner may need worked examples first, and forcing retrieval too early can discourage them.

## A closing thought

I have found that students accept the long way round once they see their own scores improve. Seeing the evidence is more persuasive than any argument, including the ones in this book.

# Appendix: Notes on the Classroom Comparison

The 2017 comparison involved four classes in one school. Two classes used rereading and two used retrieval, with the method chosen by each class. Scores were collected from a single test written by me. Two of the 212 students were absent at the one-week test and were excluded.
